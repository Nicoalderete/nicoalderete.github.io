Understand churn. Inform operations.

Reason classification, customer tenure and comparison rules to make churn reporting useful for operations.

The problem and my responsibility

Churn needs context

Counting departures does not explain why they happen. Reasons, customer tenure and the party initiating the departure need to be considered together to guide questions and priorities.

My contribution

I work on preparing and reviewing churn reports: organising sources, reviewing categories, segmenting cases and communicating findings for management.

How I structure the analysis

1. Define the population

Separate permanent departures from temporary suspensions. Set the cutoff, period and unit of analysis; check duplicates and incomplete records before counting.

2. Review reasons

Compare the assigned category with the available description and distinguish customer-initiated from company-initiated departures. Inconsistent reasons require contextual review rather than an automatic correction.

3. Make comparable comparisons

Segment by tenure and reason. Where periods differ, normalise per day and state the complete-block selection rule. Use the median for the central case and report sample size.

4. Separate description from explanation

An association between activity and departure suggests a hypothesis; it does not establish causation. Without hours worked, daily revenue cannot establish hourly profitability.

Outcome and scope

What the work contributes

A recurring view that separates volume, composition and behaviour, with explicit criteria for discussing management priorities.

What is not claimed

No customer data is published, and no reduction in churn or causal effect is attributed to this analysis. The demo code is a public reconstruction, not the internal system.
